## 2. Enemy and AI Design: The Designer's View

### 2.1 The core stance: AI that is fun, not smart

Game AI exists to produce an experience for the player, not to win. The designer's job is to decide what the player should feel when facing an enemy (threatened, outwitted, powerful, hunted, overwhelmed) and then specify behavior that produces that feeling readably.

Principles **[heuristic]**:
1. **Legibility beats sophistication.** If the player cannot tell why the enemy did something, clever behavior reads as random or cheating. Smart behavior the player never perceives is wasted production.
2. **Enemies are puzzles with a fair answer.** Each enemy asks a question (move, block, flank, prioritize a target, break line of sight). The player should be able to find the answer within a few encounters.
3. **Fail visibly, fail on purpose.** Enemies that miss their first shots, announce attacks, and leave openings give the player a rhythm. A perfect-aim enemy is not "harder", it is less readable.
4. **Pace the pressure.** Mean attack rate across all enemies matters more than any one enemy's strength. See aggression management in section 4.
5. **Cheat, but never be caught.** Hidden advantages (extra perception range, rubber-banded speed in racers) are legitimate tools when they serve the experience and are invisible or clearly telegraphed. Visible cheating (enemy sees through a wall with no explanation) breaks trust.
6. **Make the AI's state observable.** Barks, animation, color, posture and sound should communicate alert level, intent and target.

### 2.2 Enemy roles and archetypes

Think in roles (what question does this enemy ask?), then dress them in theme. A role is defined by how it changes the player's movement, targeting or resource decisions.

| Role | Question it asks the player | Typical behavior | Counter-pressure | Example families |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Grunt / fodder** | "Can you handle volume?" | Simple approach and attack, low health | Area attacks, positioning | Zombies, drones, mooks |
| **Rusher / charger** | "Can you handle threat closing in?" | Fast approach, melee, committed attack | Dodge timing, crowd control | Chargers, bats, suicide units |
| **Ranged / sniper** | "Can you manage exposure?" | Holds distance, telegraphed shot | Cover, line-of-sight breaking, closing gap | Archers, turrets, snipers |
| **Tank / bruiser** | "Can you sustain damage or find the weak point?" | High health/armor, slow, heavy attacks | Weak spots, flanking, burst | Armored brutes, shield bearers |
| **Support / buffer / healer** | "Can you prioritize targets?" | Heals, shields or buffs others, stays behind | Target priority, reach | Medics, shamans |
| **Flanker / assassin** | "Do you check your sides?" | Circles, attacks from behind, audible tell | Awareness, audio cues, back-protection | Stalkers, cloaked enemies |
| **Zoner / area denial** | "Can you read space?" | Creates hazard zones, forces movement | Mobility, interrupt | Mortars, trap layers, fire emitters |
| **Summoner / spawner** | "Do you kill the source or the product?" | Produces minions | Burst the source, control the flow | Nests, necromancers |
| **Disruptor** | "Will you break your plan?" | Disables abilities, knocks back, blinds | Cleanse, positioning | Stunners, silencers |
| **Elite / mini-boss** | "Can you combine all skills?" | Grunt behavior plus one extra mechanic and larger health | Everything learned so far | Champions, named enemies |

Design rules **[heuristic]**:
- **Orthogonal differentiation.** Each role should demand a different player response. Three enemies that all say "shoot it until dead" are one enemy with different skins (see `03`, section 6.3).
- **Combination is where depth lives.** Individually simple enemies create rich encounters when combined (a sniper forcing cover plus a rusher forcing movement out of cover). Design the pair matrix, not just the list.
- **Introduce, isolate, combine, twist** (`05`, section on onboarding): first appearance in a safe context, then mixed with another role, then in a hostile arena.
- **A role needs a visual and audio silhouette.** Players should identify the role in under a second, at distance, in chaos. Distinct shape, color, motion and sound are all legitimate channels.
- **Counter variety.** If the player has one tool that counters everything, enemy roles collapse. Make sure at least two counters exist per role and that none counters all roles.

### 2.3 Telegraphing and readability

A **telegraph** is any signal given before an attack lands that lets the player respond. The standard sequence is anticipation, strike, recovery (the same animation principles used in game feel; see `03`, section 10).

Telegraph design parameters **[heuristic]**:
| Parameter | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **Windup duration** | Must exceed human reaction time plus input latency plus the time the dodge/block takes. Many action games use windups of several hundred milliseconds for basic attacks and longer for heavy ones; tune in playtests, do not copy a number. |
| **Channels** | Use at least two: visual (pose, glow, ground decal) and audio (grunt, charge-up). The second channel supports players who miss the first, including players with low vision or hearing differences. |
| **Consistency** | The same attack always has the same tell. Variance in timing (delays, feints) is a late-game tool and should be introduced after the base timing is learned. |
| **Off-screen threats** | Attacks from outside the view need an audio cue and ideally a directional indicator. Otherwise they feel unfair. |
| **Hit vs. tell contrast** | The tell should be visually louder than ambient effects so it is not buried in particle noise. |
| **Recovery window** | A big attack should leave an exploitable recovery. Risk/reward: the more committed the attack, the longer the opening. |

Fairness check: "Could a player who has never seen this attack, reacting only to the tell, survive it once they have been hit by it?" If the answer is "only by guessing", it is a trial-and-error trap rather than a skill test. Trial-and-error is acceptable in some genres (fast retry roguelike-style, bullet hell) when death teaches and retry is quick.

Common readability failures:
- Telegraph hidden behind the player character or UI.
- Too many simultaneous telegraphs, none of which stand out.
- Enemy animation that does not match the actual hitbox (late hit, early hit, wrong range).
- Attacks that track the player through the entire windup (homing), removing the dodge decision. If tracking is used, specify when tracking stops (the "commit point").

### 2.4 Enemy design spec (template)

For each enemy, write:
1. **Role and question** (one sentence).
2. **Fantasy/theme** and silhouette notes.
3. **Stats**: health, speed, damage, attack rate, range; keep stats as variables in data, not code.
4. **Behaviors** as states with entry/exit conditions (see section 3).
5. **Telegraphs** per attack: windup, channels, commit point, recovery.
6. **Counters**: what tools or tactics beat it; what does it counter in the player kit.
7. **Combinations**: which other enemies it pairs with, which it must not pair with early.
8. **Failure modes**: stuck on geometry, loses target, clusters, can be exploited by cheesing (for example, out-of-reach ranged attacks).
9. **Metrics**: expected time-to-kill range, damage dealt per encounter, deaths attributed to it (`12-playtesting-and-analytics.md`).

## 3. AI Architectures a Designer Must Understand

You do not need to implement these, but you need to know which one fits the behavior you are asking for, what authoring cost it implies, and where it fails. Standard programmer references are Millington and Funge (2009), Buckland (2004) and the free *Game AI Pro* chapters (Rabin, ed.).

### 3.1 Comparison

| Architecture | Idea | Designer-facing strengths | Weaknesses | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Finite state machine (FSM)** | Enemy is always in one state (Idle, Patrol, Chase, Attack); transitions have conditions | Easy to read, debug, and tune; maps directly to animations and barks | Transitions explode as states grow ("state spaghetti"); hard to reuse; poor at running behaviors in parallel | Simple enemies, UI-like logic, early prototypes |
| **Hierarchical FSM** | States contain sub-states (Combat -> Approach / Strafe / Retreat) | Reuse of common transitions; manages complexity | Still rigid; transitions between hierarchy levels get tangled | Mid-complexity enemies |
| **Behavior tree (BT)** | A tree of selectors (try children in order until one succeeds), sequences (run children in order), decorators (conditions, cooldowns), and leaf actions | Modular, reusable subtrees, designer-readable graph, easy to add priority behaviors | Reactive logic needs careful decorators; can become huge; "priority" semantics are not intuitive to everyone | Most action, shooter and RPG enemies; common in commercial engines |
| **Utility AI** | Each possible action scores itself from considerations (health, distance, ammo) via curves; highest score wins | Smooth, context-sensitive choices; natural fit for sims and tactical choices; behaviors are tuned by curves, not hand-written rules | Hard to predict and debug; tuning many curves can be opaque; can oscillate without hysteresis | Sims, strategy, companions, systemic NPCs (see Mark 2009) |
| **GOAP (goal-oriented action planning)** | Agent has goals and a set of actions with preconditions and effects; a planner searches for an action sequence that meets the goal | Emergent routes to goals; actions are modular and reusable; designers add actions without rewiring | Plans can surprise designers; harder to guarantee exact scripted outcomes; planning cost | Tactical shooters, systemic stealth, sandbox NPCs |
| **HTN (hierarchical task network)** | Planner decomposes high-level tasks into subtasks using authored methods | Designer controls structure while keeping flexibility | Authoring effort for methods; less emergent than GOAP | Cases where authored intent matters most |
| **Scripted / authored** | Explicit timelines | Total control, cinematic | Brittle, no adaptation | Bosses, set pieces |
| **Machine-learned** | Policies trained by RL or imitation | Can find non-obvious strategies | Hard to direct, tune and explain; testing cost | Mostly research, bots, playtest agents (see `12`) |

**[heuristic]** pick order: start with a FSM or behavior tree; add utility scoring for decisions that depend on many continuous variables; reach for planners only when you need many agents to find different routes to goals and your team can afford the debugging.

### 3.2 Finite state machines

Example, a melee grunt:

```
Idle --(sees player)--> Chase
Chase --(within 2 m)--> Attack
Attack --(animation done)--> Recover
Recover --(0.6 s)--> Chase
Chase --(lost sight 5 s)--> Search
Search --(timeout)--> Idle
Any --(health = 0)--> Die
```

Designer tips:
- Every state should have an **exit**; avoid states that can only exit on a condition that may never occur. Add timeouts.
- Add **hysteresis** (enter chase at 10 m, leave at 14 m) to prevent flicker at thresholds.
- Name states after player-observable behavior, not code ("Winding up", not "State3").
- Keep a table of transitions in the spec; a missing row is a bug waiting to be found.

### 3.3 Behavior trees

Example, a ranged soldier (priority from top, first success wins):

```
Selector
  Sequence [Low health?]  -> FindCover -> Heal/Bandage
  Sequence [Has line of sight AND has attack token] -> Aim -> Fire burst -> Reposition
  Sequence [Player in melee range] -> Retreat step -> Fire
  Sequence [No line of sight] -> Advance to flank position
  Idle / Peek from cover
```

Designer tips:
- Priorities encode personality: swap the order of "take cover" and "attack" and the soldier changes from cautious to aggressive.
- Use **cooldown decorators** to prevent repeating the same move, and **random weights** among siblings for variety (with limits so that two identical telegraphs never repeat three times).
- Parameterize: expose aggression, preferred range, reaction time and accuracy as data per enemy type and per difficulty.
- Keep trees shallow and named; a tree no one can read is a FSM disguised.

### 3.4 Utility AI

Each action gets a score between 0 and 1 from "considerations" mapped through curves. Example for a companion deciding what to do:

| Action | Considerations | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Heal ally | ally health low (steep curve), own cooldown ready, no enemy adjacent | Score spikes below ~30% ally health **[heuristic example]** |
| Attack target | enemy in range, enemy threat, own ammo | Baseline action, never below a small floor |
| Take cover | incoming fire, own health, distance to cover | Increases when health decreases |
| Follow player | distance to player (rises beyond leash) | Prevents wandering off |

Designer concerns: add **momentum** (bonus to the current action) to avoid dithering; log the winning action and its score so playtests can show why an NPC did something; cap the number of considerations per action (a handful) so tuning stays tractable. Dave Mark's *Behavioral Mathematics for Game AI* is the standard treatment of the math.

### 3.5 GOAP and F.E.A.R.

Jeff Orkin's presentation on F.E.A.R.'s AI (GDC 2006; see References) describes a small set of states for the agents (essentially go-to, animate, use smart object) with a planner choosing the sequence of actions that satisfies goals such as killing the player or staying in cover. The lesson for designers is the division of labor: designers define **goals** and **actions** (with preconditions and effects) while the planner assembles them. The visible result is soldiers that flank, flush the player from cover, and call out intentions to one another.

A key design point reported from that work, and worth borrowing: **the NPCs announced what they were doing** (dialogue barks like calling for suppression or flanking) so that the player perceived intelligence. Communicating intent is often a cheaper way to feel smart than adding more logic. Use it deliberately but keep barks consistent with real behavior; a bark that lies teaches the player to ignore all barks.

GOAP design considerations **[heuristic]**:
- Keep the action set small and orthogonal; every new action multiplies possible plans.
- Assign **costs** to actions so the planner prefers the behaviors you want to see most.
- Write tests that run plans in controlled scenarios; planners fail quietly by choosing odd but valid plans.
- Provide an override layer for scripted sequences and boss moments.

### 3.6 Perception, memory and targeting

AI decisions depend on what the agent knows. Specify:
- **Senses**: sight (range, field of view, line of sight, light level), hearing (loudness radius per action), touch/damage, shared alerts (allies calling out).
- **Memory**: how long the last known position persists; whether the agent searches there; how information decays.
- **Target selection**: nearest, most threatening, lowest health, current objective. Different enemies should use different rules; a "always target the player" enemy is simple, "target whoever damaged me last" yields interesting taunt mechanics in co-op.
- **Reaction time**: a deliberate delay (a few hundred milliseconds is typical) between perception and action keeps AI humanlike and fair **[heuristic]**.

### 3.7 Navigation and spatial reasoning

Most AI failures players notice are navigation failures: stuck in doorways, running in circles, getting hit while pathing through hazards. Specify: nav-mesh coverage and jump/climb links, avoidance between agents, cover-point authoring (or automatic cover queries), "tactical positions" (flank spots, high ground), and fallback behavior when no path exists (play a taunt, throw something, retreat). Build test arenas that deliberately break navigation (narrow corridors, moving platforms, dynamic obstacles).

### 3.8 Pitfalls of AI architecture choices

- Picking a heavy planner for six simple enemy types.
- Letting designers tune behavior by editing code. Data-driven parameters, in-game debug overlays (state, target, scores, perception cones) and logging are mandatory tools.
- No difficulty plan: difficulty should change attack frequency, reaction time, aggression and accuracy first, not only health (which makes fights longer, not harder).
- Letting emergent behavior ship without a "reproduce this" tool (record AI state per frame for bug reports).

## 4. Aggression Management, Directors and Stealth AI

### 4.1 Attack tokens and aggression management

When many enemies can attack at once, the player faces unreadable chaos or an unwinnable damage spike. **Attack tokens** (also called attack slots, aggression tickets or "circling" systems) limit how many enemies may actively attack simultaneously. Enemies without a token take non-lethal roles: circle, taunt, advance slowly, strafe, or wait their turn.

How it works **[general technique]**:
1. A manager holds N tokens (N may vary per difficulty or encounter).
2. An enemy that wants to attack requests a token; if none is free, it performs a "waiting" behavior.
3. After the attack and recovery, the enemy returns the token. Tokens can be prioritized (a stronger enemy or the one waiting longest gets the next).
4. Optional: weight tokens (a heavy attack costs two).

Why designers like it:
- **Controls the threat curve** with one number. Raise N for later chapters.
- **Keeps fights readable**: the player always has a small set of active threats.
- **Creates choreography**: the crowd looks menacing but acts like a sequence of duels. Brawlers in the Batman: Arkham style, where a crowd surrounds the player and attacks come one or a few at a time with a visible counter prompt, are the commonly cited example of this feel.
- **Supports variety**: ranged units can have a separate token pool from melee so both roles stay active.

Doom (2016) is often cited for the opposite twist: aggression management that **pushes the player forward** (enemies are designed so that staying in cover is punished and killing enemies, with glory kills, rewards health and ammo). The lesson is that enemy behavior, resource drops and level layout combine to create the intended player posture (aggressive). When you describe this, frame it as an example of resource-for-aggression design rather than as a specific implementation, since internal details of these games are mostly not public.

Pitfalls **[heuristic]**:
- Players notice when enemies "politely wait". Give waiting enemies things to do (strafing, feints, cover-peeking, taunt animations) and occasionally allow a surprise attack.
- Tokens that never time out can deadlock when the token holder is stuck. Add a timeout and return tokens on death, stun or loss of target.
- Ranged enemies exempt from tokens can still overwhelm; apply separate caps to ranged fire.
- In co-op, token pools should scale with player count or the game becomes trivial.

### 4.2 Director systems

A **director** is a high-level AI that controls the pacing of encounters rather than individual enemies. The best-known example is the AI Director in Left 4 Dead, described by Mike Booth in "The AI Systems of Left 4 Dead" (Valve, 2009; see References). Principles reported there and widely adopted:
- Model the player's **intensity** (recent damage taken, nearby threats, kills) as a measure; spawn pressure when intensity is low and give relief when it is high. This produces a **build up, peak, relax** rhythm (the sawtooth in SKILL.md section 4.2).
- Control **what** spawns, **where** (outside the player's view), and **when**, from authored pools so quality stays high.
- Vary resource placement and special enemies per run to raise replayability.

Design considerations **[heuristic]**:
| Parameter | Example use |
|---|---|
| Intensity decay rate | Slower decay means longer relief periods |
| Peak duration | How long a wave is sustained before forced relief |
| Spawn budget per phase | Caps total enemy cost, not just count |
| Player-relative spawn rules | Never spawn in view or within a minimum distance |
| Skill signals | Inputs like deaths, health at end of fight, ammo, time to clear |
| Authored override | Scripted moments and boss fights suppress director spawns |

Pitfalls: director "rubber banding" that players detect and exploit (for example, deliberately staying wounded to reduce pressure); a director with no authored floor that can produce boring runs; opaque director decisions that feel like cheating. Show the logic in debug and cap how much the director adapts. See `05-progression-difficulty-onboarding.md` on dynamic difficulty adjustment ethics and transparency.

### 4.3 Stealth AI: perception, awareness meters and alert states

Stealth games are AI-legibility games: the player must read what guards know and predict what they will do.

**Awareness model** (common structure; implement as a number plus thresholds):
| State | Meaning | Guard behavior | Player-facing feedback |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unaware / idle** | Normal routine | Patrol, idle animations | Neutral posture, routine barks |
| **Suspicious** | Something odd (noise, glimpse, missing guard) | Stop, face source, investigate slowly | Rising meter, "huh?" bark, UI icon |
| **Searching** | Knows an intruder exists, not where | Search last known position, check hiding places | Different music layer, flashlight sweeps |
| **Alert / combat** | Sees or knows the player | Pursue, call allies, attack | Alarm, clear UI state, combat music |
| **Returning / cooldown** | Lost the player | Search for a time, then return to routine, possibly with heightened vigilance | Music relaxes, bark "must have been nothing" |

Design rules **[heuristic]**:
- **Gradual detection is more forgiving and more fun than binary detection.** An awareness meter (fills with time in view, scales with distance, light, movement speed, stance) lets the player choose to retreat before being seen. Binary instant detection works for lethal, one-shot-kill designs (arcade or horror) but should be a deliberate choice.
- **Expose the rules.** Players should know what makes noise, how light affects detection, how far cones reach, what a guard's field of view is. Show cones in a map or tool, or teach through feedback.
- **Persistent consequences** (guards remember, doors stay locked, patrols double) create tension but increase the cost of failure; offer recovery paths.
- **Give guards routines** that leave windows of opportunity, and make routines vary slightly between runs if replayability matters.
- **Information sharing**: decide if alerts propagate (a shout, a radio, a found body). Propagation range and speed define how much the player can improvise.
- **Fail-forward**: being detected should change the game (combat, chase, escape) rather than simply reload, unless the genre wants a fail-state.
- **Sound design is half of stealth** (section 9).

Test: show testers a guard and ask them to predict, aloud, what it will do next. If they cannot, the awareness model is not legible.

### 4.4 Group tactics and squad AI

For squads, specify roles and communication:
- **Roles** (suppressor, flanker, rusher, support) assigned dynamically by a squad coordinator, not each unit independently.
- **Squad objectives**: hold area, push, regroup, pursue; each with entry conditions.
- **Formation or slot systems** for approach geometry (slots around the player keep melee units from stacking on one point).
- **Cover and flank queries**: score tactical positions by exposure to the player, distance, and ally spacing.
- Surface intent with barks and animation so players learn counters (for example, "grenade out" tells the player to move).

## 5. Companion AI and Boss Design

### 5.1 Companion AI

Companions amplify the fantasy (partner, protector, pet) and are often judged harshly because they are in the player's face constantly.

Design principles **[heuristic]**:
1. **Never be a burden**: do not block doorways, trigger traps, wake enemies, or die so often that the player must babysit. Give invulnerability, a downed state with a revive, or auto-recover.
2. **Never steal the fun**: avoid solving puzzles or killing key enemies before the player can. Offer hints on a timer or request rather than doing the thing.
3. **Be useful but not dominant**: reduce effectiveness so that the player remains the main contributor; the companion should create openings and combos.
4. **Be legible**: companions should signal what they are doing (calling targets, announcing cooldowns).
5. **Follow with sensible pathing**: keep a leash radius, teleport or fade when stuck, avoid standing in the player's line of fire, give way in narrow places.
6. **Support player verbs**: let the player issue simple commands (hold, attack target, use ability) when the genre calls for tactics; pair commands with predictable responses.
7. **Make them characters**: ambient dialogue, reactions to the player's choices, relationship states (`07-narrative-design.md`).
8. **Tune per difficulty and player skill**: the companion can compensate when the player is struggling (revive faster) without announcing it.

Failure modes: friendly fire that frustrates, bad stealth behavior (companion charges into a sneaking player), contextless dialogue repetition, taking aggro in ways the player cannot control, and AI that cannot handle the level's custom traversal.

### 5.2 Boss design

A boss is a **final exam**: it asks the player to demonstrate skills the game has taught, in a heightened, memorable setting.

**Structure**
1. **Tell the story of the fight**: arena, music, intro, silhouette, and title card communicate stakes.
2. **Phases**: each phase changes the question. Common triggers are health thresholds or vulnerability windows. A phase should add a new attack pattern, move set, arena change, or twist, not just more health or damage.
3. **Pattern vocabulary**: 4-8 attacks per phase is a typical starting range **[heuristic]**, each with a clear tell, a defined counter, and an opening. Combine them into sequences with variation, but keep each sequence predictable enough to be learned.
4. **Learning curve inside the fight**: early phase teaches (slow, forgiving), middle tests (combinations), late phase mixes everything (speed up, remove safe spots), possibly with a final "desperation" pattern.
5. **Vulnerability windows**: the boss must become damageable in a way the player can create or anticipate. Without windows, damage is blocked by tedium instead of skill.
6. **Fair failure**: the player should know why they died. Show the cause (clear hit feedback, replay, tip on load screen).
7. **Quick retry**: short run-back or instant restart. A 90-second walk to the boss after every death turns a learning loop into a penalty (`05`).
8. **Checkpointing at phase boundaries** (optional), especially for long fights.

**Boss pattern table (example for a melee action game)**
| Phase | Health band | Patterns | New question | Opening |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100-60% | Slow overhead slam; side sweep; jump-stomp | Basic dodge timing | After slam recovery |
| 2 | 60-25% | Phase 1 patterns at +20% speed; add projectile volley with 1 s windup; adds arena hazard | Spatial control while dodging | After volley, if the player is at mid-range |
| 3 | 25-0% | Combo chains of two patterns; desperation AOE with large tell | Pattern recognition under pressure | After AOE; longer recovery |

(These bands and speed increases are illustrative; tune by playtest.)

**Boss patterns to avoid**: unavoidable damage, phase transitions that are long unskippable cutscenes on retries, random patterns with no telegraph, "damage sponge" health bars where the only change is time, hitboxes that do not match visuals, and bosses that require a build/ability the player may not have.

**Variations**: puzzle bosses (find the weakness), environmental bosses (use the arena), chase bosses, multi-stage endurance, duel bosses (mirror the player's verbs), and bosses with adds (target priority).

**Co-op and scaling bosses**: scale health, number of attack targets, and add pressure instead of simply multiplying health. Provide a role for each player (tank, DPS, support) if the genre allows.

**Tuning workflow**: prototype with a dummy boss to test arena and camera, script one phase, run playtests where observers log "deaths per pattern", adjust the worst offender first, and track the share of players who clear within a target number of attempts (set the target per audience; there is no universal number).

## 6. Procedural Generation: Design Goals and When to Use It

### 6.1 What PCG is for

Procedural content generation (PCG) is the algorithmic creation of game content (levels, maps, items, quests, enemies, textures, music) with limited or indirect human input. Shaker, Togelius and Nelson (2016) organize the field around what content is generated, how it is controlled, and how it is evaluated; the book is free online (see References).

Reasons to use PCG **[design goals]**:
| Goal | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Replayability** | Each run differs, so mastery of systems, not memorization of content, drives play | Roguelikes (Spelunky, Slay the Spire map, Dead Cells) |
| **Scale** | Vast content with a small team | Large explorable worlds, modular dungeons |
| **Emergent stories** | Unique situations players can talk about | Dwarf Fortress-style worlds, Minecraft terrain |
| **Adaptivity** | Content tuned to the player (difficulty, taste) | Director spawn choices, item drops |
| **Cost and iteration** | Faster iteration on level structure | Generator-based prototyping |
| **Unpredictability** | Break memorization in competitive or shared-seed formats | Daily challenge runs |

Reasons not to **[heuristic]**:
- The core experience depends on **authored emotional beats** (narrative set pieces, precisely tuned platforming challenges).
- You cannot **test** the output. Content that cannot be validated tends to include broken or boring instances.
- Your team has no tools or time to build, visualize and debug generators. Generators are software with their own bug surface.
- A fixed handcrafted set is enough to supply the hours the audience wants.

### 6.2 The 10,000 bowls of oatmeal problem

Kate Compton (see References) describes this: you can make 10,000 bowls of oatmeal, each with a different arrangement of grains, and the player will perceive them as the same bowl. **Variation in raw parameters is not variety in experience.** What matters is **perceptual differentiability** and whether differences make a difference to play.

Implications for designers:
- Define the **expressive range**: the space of outputs the generator can produce (Shaker et al. discuss visualizing expressive range by plotting metrics such as linearity or leniency across many samples). Check whether outputs cluster in a narrow band.
- Vary **kinds**, not just **amounts**: different structures, different challenge types, different rules in effect, not just different numbers.
- Create **landmarks and identity**: memorable, nameable features (a vault, a lake level, a shop type) help perception.
- Use **meaningful constraints** (themes, rules) so outputs have character, then vary the constraint set per run (modifiers, biomes, mutators).
- Insert **authored rarity**: special rooms, events or encounters that appear rarely and feel hand-made.

### 6.3 Variety versus authored quality

| Pole | Gains | Loses |
|---|---|---|
| More authored | Pacing, set-piece quality, narrative control, balance | Replayability, content volume |
| More generated | Replayability, scale | Predictable pacing, coherent identity, quality floor |

Practical stance **[heuristic]**: **generate the arrangement, author the pieces.** Authored chunks, rooms, encounters and items, composed by rules, usually beat pure noise-driven generation in quality, and authoring cost goes into pieces that appear many times.

### 6.4 Generator design questions

Ask before building:
1. What is the **unit** of content (room, level, item, quest)?
2. What **experience** should every output deliver (guaranteed minimum)? What should vary?
3. What are the **hard constraints** (solvable, fair, within resource bounds) and **soft goals** (interesting, paced)?
4. How will you **evaluate** outputs (automated metrics, bots, human review)?
5. What **controls** does the designer have (parameters, templates, seeds, modifiers)?
6. What is the **failure plan** (reject and regenerate, repair, fallback to authored)?

## 7. Procedural Generation: Techniques at the Design Level

You need to understand what each technique produces and how much control it gives you, not how to code it. See Shaker et al. (2016), chapters on each family, for detail.

### 7.1 Technique overview

| Technique | What it does | Control | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Templates / chunk assembly** | Assemble hand-authored pieces (rooms, rooms-within-grids) into levels | High; direct authoring | Platformer and dungeon levels, encounter sets | Repetition if few chunks; seams between chunks |
| **Grammars (shape, graph, L-systems)** | Rewrite rules expand a high-level structure into a detailed one (for example, a mission graph expanded into rooms and keys) | Medium-high; structure rules are explicit | Missions, quests, buildings, dungeon topology | Rules can be hard to write for "interesting"; combinatorial explosion |
| **Noise functions (Perlin, simplex, Worley)** | Smooth pseudo-random fields | Low-medium; parameters for scale, octaves, thresholds | Terrain, heightmaps, biomes, resource distribution | Looks generic without authored structure on top |
| **Cellular automata** | Local rules iterate on a grid | Low-medium | Caves, organic regions | Disconnected regions; need cleanup and connectivity pass |
| **Space partitioning (BSP, rooms and corridors)** | Split space and place rooms, connect them | Medium | Classic dungeons | Samey layouts without extra rules |
| **Random walk / drunkard's walk** | An agent moves and carves | Low-medium | Caves, rivers, winding tunnels | Uneven coverage |
| **Agent-based (digging agents, simulated actors)** | Multiple agents make decisions to place content | Medium | Organic worlds, history simulation, settlements | Hard to predict, tune and debug |
| **Wave Function Collapse (WFC)** | Constraint propagation from example patterns: pick the lowest-entropy cell, choose a tile, propagate adjacency rules | Medium; control through example input and constraints | Tile maps and 3D modular environments that look like an exemplar | Contradictions require restarts; global structure (paths, goals) not guaranteed |
| **Constraint solving / SAT / answer-set** | Declare constraints and let a solver find a layout | High on constraints | Puzzles, layouts requiring guarantees | Performance; designers must express intent as constraints |
| **Search-based (evolutionary) PCG** | Generate candidates, score with a fitness function, evolve | Medium; control via fitness function | Balancing, level tuning, generating puzzles with target difficulty | Fitness design is hard; local optima |
| **Simulation / history generation** | Run a world simulation to create state and lore | Low; emergent | Narrative texture, worlds with believable history | Output may be boring unless the simulation has interesting drivers |
| **Mixed-initiative / human-in-loop** | Tools that suggest, designers curate | High | Production content pipelines | Needs tool UX |

### 7.2 Spelunky-style template generation (design view)

A well-known structure, discussed in Derek Yu's *Spelunky* (Boss Fight Books) and in numerous community analyses: the level is a grid of rooms; a random **solution path** from top to bottom is chosen first, rooms along that path are drawn from templates that guarantee passage (for example, rooms with openings left/right/down), and remaining rooms are filled with other templates, with randomly placed special features and traps within the templates. Verify exact details in the source before quoting any numbers or implementation specifics; here the point is the pattern:
1. Pick the **guaranteed path** first (solvability).
2. Fill path cells with **compatible authored chunks** (quality).
3. Fill the rest with **flavor chunks** (variety).
4. Layer **dynamic content** (enemies, items, hazards) with rules.

This pattern is a template for most grid-based generation.

### 7.3 Designing the template library

For chunk-based systems:
- Tag each chunk with **connectors** (open exits), **difficulty**, **role** (fight, rest, puzzle, reward), **theme**, and **required abilities**.
- Aim for chunks with **internal variation** (swap enemy groups, props, small layouts) so the same chunk is not recognizable after a few runs.
- Test the **transition rules**: which chunks can neighbor each other without an unfair situation (for example, a spike pit immediately after a blind drop).
- Track **frequency**: record which chunks appear most; rebalance weights so players see authored rarity as a treat.

### 7.4 Wave Function Collapse in practice

WFC (Maxim Gumin's open-source implementation; see References) is popular for modular worlds. Designer takeaways:
- It produces **local coherence** (neighbors fit) but not **global design** (reachability, pacing). Combine with a path or zoning pass: place the critical path and landmarks first as constraints, then let WFC fill the rest.
- You control the result through the **tile set**, **adjacency rules**, **weights**, and **pre-collapsed cells** (pins).
- It can fail by reaching contradictions; plan restarts or backtracking, and accept a retry budget.

### 7.5 Parameter spaces and expressive range

For each generator, create a table of parameters, ranges and the player-visible effect. Sample thousands of outputs and plot design-relevant metrics (path length, number of enemies, resource density, branching factor). If the scatter plot is a blob, you have oatmeal; if it contains outliers that are unfair, add constraints.

## 8. Solvability, Pacing, Seeds and Authored-Plus-Generated

### 8.1 Guaranteeing solvability

Unwinnable or unfair content destroys trust. Strategies, ordered from most to least reliable:
1. **Constructive guarantee**: generate in a way that solvability follows from the construction (path first, key before lock, abilities needed placed upstream of gates).
2. **Verification**: after generation, run a **solver or bot** (flood fill for reachability, pathfinding with the player's movement rules, a simulated playthrough) and reject failing outputs.
3. **Repair**: detect issues and patch (add a connector, remove a blocking object).
4. **Fallback**: use an authored default layout when generation fails after N attempts.
5. **Player-side escape hatches**: reroll, skip, dig, teleport. These do not replace the above but soften the damage of rare failures.

Common solvability bugs:
- Key behind its own lock; ability-gated route placed before the ability is obtainable.
- Disconnected regions in cave generators.
- Required resource (key, fuel, health) not guaranteed on the main route.
- Randomized items that can make the game unwinnable (a "fair randomizer" problem, familiar from item randomizers).

Beyond binary solvability, aim for **fairness**: the best case should not be boring and the worst case should not be hopeless. Techniques: pity rules for drops, guaranteed minimum healing/options by depth, "weighted" random that adjusts based on what the player has seen, and limits on stacking negative modifiers. (See `04-balance-and-economy.md` and `design_calc.py` `prd` for pseudo-random distribution.)

### 8.2 Pacing in generated content

Generated content needs a **pacing skeleton**:
- Define **beats** (arrival, challenge, rest, reward, climax) as slots with constraints; fill slots with content that matches the role.
- Use **budgets**: total threat per area, total resources per area, with spikes planned by the skeleton.
- Use **ordering rules**: no two hard fights back-to-back unless it is a deliberate gauntlet; guarantee a rest or shop every N rooms (tune N) **[heuristic]**.
- Maintain a **difficulty curve** over depth/time (`05`). The curve is the input; the generator picks content to match.
- Layer **surprise**: allow rare deviations (an ambush in a rest room) so the skeleton does not become predictable.

Slay the Spire's map is a good study of a visible pacing skeleton: the player sees node types (fights, elites, rests, shops, events) and chooses a route, so pacing is generated but player-steerable. See `03` and `11` for related roguelike treatment.

### 8.3 Seeds, sharing and reproducibility

A **seed** is the initial value of the pseudo-random number generator. Same seed plus same generator version plus same inputs gives the same output.

Design uses:
- **Daily or weekly challenges** with a shared seed for leaderboards.
- **Sharing runs** or levels by seed (players discuss "that seed").
- **Bug reproduction** and automated testing.
- **Speedrunning and competitive** formats that require identical content.

Cautions **[heuristic]**:
- Separate RNG streams for independent subsystems (level layout, loot, combat rolls) so that a player action in one does not change generation in another. Otherwise "same seed" diverges as soon as choices differ and save-scumming exploits appear.
- Version your generator; any change breaks old seeds. Keep a version tag with shared seeds.
- Decide whether combat randomness is seeded (consistent with seeded replays) and whether re-loading a save re-rolls outcomes (anti save-scum).
- Seeds can be mined for exploits; competitive formats may need curated seeds.
- Hash string seeds so players can share readable ones.

### 8.4 Mixing authored and generated

| Pattern | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Authored chunks, generated layout** | Hand-made rooms assembled by rules | Spelunky, many roguelikes |
| **Generated skeleton, authored set pieces** | Procedural path with hand-placed key moments | Dead Cells (hand-designed room pieces assembled with some fixed special rooms), Hades (rooms chosen from authored layouts with encounter rules) |
| **Authored narrative, generated delivery** | Narrative nodes placed procedurally but written by hand | Hades' conversation system picks authored lines by state; roguelike event pools |
| **Procedural base, authored pass** | A world is generated and designers edit it | Terrain generators followed by hand-tuning |
| **Authored rules, generated content** | Designers define systems; content emerges | Dwarf Fortress-style simulation |

Check the claims about specific games before quoting implementation details; the pattern descriptions above are widely discussed but each studio's actual pipeline differs. What matters for you is the principle: **spend authoring effort where the player's attention is, and let the generator handle where variety is cheap**.

Hades as a case: combat rooms are procedurally selected and arranged with authored encounters and reward types, while a large authored narrative responds to the run's outcomes; the combination gives high replayability and strong emotional beats (see `03`, section 13.5, and `07`).

### 8.5 Testing generators

- **Mass simulation**: generate thousands of outputs nightly, run automated checks (solvable, within resource budgets, no overlaps), and log failures with the seed.
- **Metrics dashboards**: distributions of path length, encounter counts, rarity of special rooms.
- **Bot playthroughs** for difficulty estimation.
- **Human review of a random sample** and of the worst 1% by metric.
- **Seed-based bug reports**: every bug report includes the seed and generator version.

### 8.6 Pitfalls

- Treating the generator as the product; the player experiences the content, not the algorithm.
- No **debug visualization**: you cannot fix what you cannot see.
- Rare impossible states that appear in the first 100 hours of live play (test volume matters).
- Too much randomness in resources, leading to "run was lost before I made a choice" (see `11-genre-playbooks.md`, Roguelike and Roguelite pitfalls).
- Variation without meaning (section 6.2).

## 9. Audio Design for Designers

Audio is a design channel, not decoration. It communicates state, danger, direction, reward and emotion, and in many cases it is faster than vision (reaction to audio is generally quicker than to visual cues; treat as a general finding and verify per project).

### 9.1 Functional audio

Think of every sound as carrying one or more **functions**:
| Function | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Feedback** | Confirm input and outcome | Hit sounds, pickup chimes, button clicks, "denied" tones |
| **Telegraph / warning** | Announce threat or timing | Enemy windup grunt, charging whine, alarm |
| **State** | Show status | Low health heartbeat, shield recharge hum |
| **Spatial cue** | Direction and distance | Footsteps behind, distant gunfire, positional audio |
| **Identification** | Distinguish objects and enemies | Unique voice or motif per enemy type |
| **Reward / progress** | Reinforce success | Level-up fanfare, combo escalation pitch |
| **Mood / emotion** | Set tone | Ambient bed, music, reverb |
| **Narrative** | Story information | VO, audio logs, diegetic radio |
| **Rhythm / timing** | Teach timing | Metronome-like cues in timing mechanics |

Design tips **[heuristic]**:
- **Layered feedback scaled to importance** (SKILL.md section 4.8): small actions get small sounds; rare events get big, distinct ones.
- **Distinctness over realism**: sounds for critical information should be unmistakably different from each other and from ambient noise (different frequency range, rhythm or timbre).
- **Consistency**: same sound, same meaning, always. Reusing a "pickup" sound for a "damage" event confuses.
- **Escalation**: pitch, tempo or layers rise with combos, health danger or proximity to teach intensity.
- **Anticipation and release**: a short silence or drop before a big hit enhances impact.
- **Variation**: randomize pitch slightly and rotate through multiple recordings for frequent sounds to avoid fatigue; keep key information sounds consistent enough to be recognized.
- **Audio-first telegraphs** for off-screen enemies and fast attacks (section 2.3).
- **Audio latency matters**: for rhythm and action feedback, keep audio latency low and test on target hardware.

### 9.2 Spatial audio cues

- Positional audio with attenuation lets players locate sources; pan and filtering (muffling behind walls) convey occlusion.
- Reverb conveys space size and material, helping the player sense the environment.
- **Verticality** is hard with stereo; use distinct filtering or additional cues when height matters.
- Competitive shooters rely on footstep clarity: design it as a balance lever (loudness radius per movement type, surfaces, perks that dampen it).
- Stealth games use sound as a **mechanic**: noise radius visualized or tied to movement speed and surface.

### 9.3 Adaptive and interactive music

Music can track gameplay state. Two core approaches (see Collins 2008; Phillips 2014 for treatments):
| Approach | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Horizontal resequencing** | Music is cut into segments; the system chooses the next segment (or loop) based on state, transitioning at musical boundaries | Large changes between sections (explore -> combat); can use fully distinct pieces | Transitions must be authored; abrupt changes if poorly aligned |
| **Vertical layering (re-orchestration)** | Multiple synchronized stems play together; layers fade in and out with intensity | Seamless, continuous intensity control | Stems must work in any combination; less variety in structure |
| **Stingers** | Short one-shot musical hits on events | Cheap, strong punctuation | Overuse becomes noise |
| **Generative / algorithmic** | Music is generated or recombined by rules | Endless variation | Hard to control emotion and quality |
| **Beat-synced gameplay** | Gameplay events quantized to music | Strong feel in rhythm-like designs | Constrains design |

Design considerations **[heuristic]**:
- Define the **state model** that music reads (exploration, tension, combat, victory, low health, stealth alert) and the transitions between states. Map music states to the AI awareness states (section 4.3).
- Define **transition rules**: wait for next bar, crossfade over N beats, cut with stinger. Specify, do not leave it to a default.
- Avoid **thrashing**: add hysteresis (combat music holds for a minimum time after the last threat) so it does not flicker.
- Keep **loop fatigue** in mind: long exploration phases need long or varied beds, or music that gets out of the way.
- Use **silence** as a design tool: horror and tension rely on its contrast.
- Plan for **player agency**: players often want to mute or reduce music; sound options (separate sliders for music, SFX, voice) are a baseline (section 9.5).

### 9.4 Mixing and priority

Many sounds compete for limited attention and limited voices (channels). A **mix** with priorities prevents loss of critical information.

Priority ladder example **[heuristic]**, highest first:
1. Critical gameplay warnings and player-directed telegraphs.
2. Player feedback (own hits, damage taken, pickups).
3. Dialogue/VO that carries mission information.
4. Nearby enemy and environmental cues.
5. Music.
6. Ambience and distant/ambient detail.

Techniques:
- **Ducking**: lower music and ambience when VO or important cues play.
- **Voice limiting and prioritization**: cap simultaneous instances of the same sound (for example, many footsteps) and drop lowest priority first.
- **Dynamic range choices**: loud/quiet contrast communicates importance; avoid mastering everything loud. Offer dynamic range modes (night mode) for home listeners.
- **Frequency separation**: assign roles to frequency bands (music mid-range, key SFX cutting through) so sounds do not mask one another.
- **Test on different outputs**: speakers, headphones, TV, laptop, phone speakers.
- **Mix in context**, in the real game under busy conditions, not in an isolated audio tool.

### 9.5 Audio as accessibility

Audio accessibility works in both directions (Game Accessibility Guidelines; see References):
- **For players who cannot hear or have difficulty hearing**: subtitles (with speaker names, adjustable size, background contrast), captions for important sound effects (direction, type), visual or haptic substitutes for audio-only cues (directional damage indicators, vibration, visual awareness meter).
- **For players who cannot see or have low vision**: audio cues for navigation and UI (menu narration, sonar-style pings, distinct audio for objects), text-to-speech for menus, audio descriptions where relevant.
- **For players with sensory sensitivities**: volume sliders per category, ability to reduce sudden loud sounds, optional reduced bass/impact, optional flashing/audio spikes warnings, mono audio option.
- **Principle**: never use audio (or visuals) as the **only** channel for essential information. Provide at least two channels.
- Make audio options reachable in the first-run flow and in the pause menu.

Cross-link: `08-player-psychology-ux-accessibility.md` covers wider accessibility guidance.

### 9.6 Voice, barks and dialogue systems

- Use **barks** (short context lines) for feedback about state and AI intent (section 3.5).
- Manage **repetition**: track recent lines, apply cooldowns, provide enough variants for frequent triggers.
- Priority and interruption rules: critical VO can interrupt ambient barks; lines can be queued with timeouts so they do not play after they are irrelevant.
- Subtitles must reflect interruptions properly.

### 9.7 Examples (observational; verify details in the source before quoting)

- **Rhythm games** use audio as the primary mechanic; latency calibration is a required feature (`11`, Rhythm).
- **Shooters** use hit markers and layered hit sounds as the main feedback for confirming damage (headshot variants, kill confirmation).
- **Horror** uses sparse sound, sudden silence and diegetic sources to build dread (`11`, Horror).
- **Open-world action games** often use music that fades out during exploration and fades in with encounter intensity; the vertical layering model is a common implementation.
- **Stealth games** make audio a mechanic: noise radius, distraction items, music reflecting alert state.

### 9.8 Audio design process

1. **Audio pillars**: three or four statements (for example, "every threat is audible before it is visible").
2. **Event list**: every game event that needs sound, tagged with function and priority.
3. **State map for music** and transitions.
4. **Mix plan**: buses, ducking rules, voice limits, loudness targets.
5. **Placeholder audio early**: use temporary sounds from the first prototype; game feel is audio-dependent.
6. **Iteration with playtests**: ask "what do you think that sound meant?" to test comprehension.
7. **Accessibility pass** at the end of each milestone.

## 10. Game Theory for Designers

Game theory is the mathematics of strategic interaction. Designers do not need the formal proofs; they need the vocabulary to diagnose why a strategy dominates, why a mixed game stays interesting, and why cooperation breaks down.

### 10.1 Dominant and dominated strategies

- A strategy **strictly dominates** another if it yields a better outcome regardless of what opponents do. A **dominated** strategy should never be chosen by a rational player.
- Design implication: if one option dominates, you have a **false choice** (SKILL.md section 4.4). Fix by adding a cost, a situational counter, or a context where the other option wins.
- **Dominance is context-dependent**: a unit may dominate in the current meta but lose to a counter that is not yet discovered. Playtest with strong players or simulate (`04`, `12`).
- Use a **payoff matrix** to inspect two-option interactions.

Example payoff matrix (illustrative numbers): two fighters pick Attack or Block each round.

| | Opponent attacks | Opponent blocks |
|---|---|---|
| **You attack** | both take damage (0, 0) | you are blocked and punished (-1, +1) |
| **You block** | you counter and gain (+1, -1) | nothing happens (0, 0) |

No pure option dominates; the interesting decision is the guess, which links to mixed strategies below.

### 10.2 Nash equilibrium

A **Nash equilibrium** is a set of strategies where no player can improve their outcome by changing only their own strategy. Designer takeaways:
- A game can have **multiple equilibria**, some bad for everyone. Designers influence which equilibrium players find through incentives and information.
- A **stable equilibrium** in a competitive game often means the **meta has been solved**: all players use the same few strategies. This is not always bad, but a solved meta ends exploration. Rock-paper-scissors-like structures produce cycles rather than a fixed point and keep a meta moving.
- Equilibrium analysis is a **diagnostic**: if your analysis says every player should do the same thing, expect a degenerate game.

### 10.3 Mixed strategies and rock-paper-scissors

When no pure strategy is best, rational play is **randomizing** between options in specific proportions (a mixed strategy). In rock-paper-scissors the equilibrium is to pick each option equally often. Predictability is exploitable.

Real-world analogues: penalty kicks in football (kicker and goalkeeper choose a direction; if either is predictable the other exploits). Whether professional players' choices match mixed-strategy predictions has been studied in the economics literature; treat any specific claim as something to check in the original paper before citing.

Designer applications:
- **Guessing games with simultaneous commitment** (fighting-game mix-ups, simultaneous action selection in tabletop games, bluffing in poker-like games) are mixed-strategy games. Their depth comes from information about the opponent's tendencies.
- **Make randomization a skill**: provide enough options and consequences that mixing is non-trivial. If one option's expected value always exceeds the others, there is no mixing.
- **Balance by payoff structure**: in a symmetric hand game the equilibrium mix depends on payoffs. Adjust payoffs to move the optimal frequency; a designer can make a "risky but powerful" option correct to play 20% of the time rather than 0% or 100%.
- **Counter triangles** (strong against A, weak against B) are intransitive balance; see `03` section 6.4.

### 10.4 Yomi (David Sirlin)

**Yomi** (Japanese for "reading") is the ability to predict the opponent's actions. In *Playing to Win*, chapter "Spies of the Mind" (verified URL in References), Sirlin describes yomi as extremely valuable in competitive play; a common formulation is the idea of **layers**: at layer 0 you play the move you want; at layer 1 you anticipate your opponent's likely move and counter it; at layer 2 you anticipate that your opponent anticipates you; and so on, with the practical advice that the useful depth is limited because opponents rarely go deep and you only need to be one layer ahead (check Sirlin's text before quoting specifics).

Designing for yomi **[heuristic]**:
- Provide **hard reads vs. safe options**: a safe option that costs expected value, and risky reads that pay.
- Make **information about tendencies** available through history (visible previous moves, replays) so that players can learn opponents.
- Maintain **meaningful counters** (the triangle) at several time scales: attack beats throw, throw beats block, block beats attack. Fighting game design (`11`, Fighting) is the best study.
- Limit **randomness** that obscures reads: yomi games usually need low output randomness so that "I read right" translates into a reliable result.
- Avoid **pure guess** situations with huge swings and no information; they feel arbitrary. Add information (frame data, prior behavior) or shrink the stakes.

### 10.5 Prisoner's dilemma, co-op and traitor games

The **prisoner's dilemma** is a payoff structure where individual rational choice (defect) leads to a worse collective outcome than mutual cooperation, even though each is tempted to defect.

Table: classic payoffs (illustrative, higher is better)

| | Other cooperates | Other defects |
|---|---|---|
| **You cooperate** | 3, 3 | 0, 5 |
| **You defect** | 5, 0 | 1, 1 |

Designer applications:
- **Shared-resource games** (a common pool, a public good) are social dilemmas. Players contribute less than optimal unless the game gives reasons: repeated play, reputation, communication, enforcement, or side deals.
- **Repeated games** (the same players meet again) support cooperation (tit-for-tat style strategies) because defection can be punished. Persistent communities and reputations make cooperation more likely; anonymous one-shot matches make defection more likely (`10-multiplayer-and-social.md`).
- **Traitor/hidden-role games** (Werewolf-style, Battlestar Galactica style, Among Us style) create dilemmas through hidden information: players cannot tell cooperators from defectors, so trust is the resource. Design levers: how much information leaks through actions, how many traitors, how accusation works, how a wrongly accused player is treated, and whether the traitor has a meaningful plan other than sabotage.
- **Cooperative games with scarce shared resources** invite free-riding and alpha-player behavior (`15-tabletop-design.md`).
- **Rewards structure**: if individual rewards conflict with team success, a social dilemma emerges (loot rolls, kill steals, score-based rankings in team games). Be deliberate about whether you want that tension.

### 10.6 Other concepts worth knowing

| Concept | Meaning | Design use |
|---|---|---|
| **Zero-sum vs. non-zero-sum** | One player's gain is another's loss vs. mutual gains possible | Zero-sum creates direct conflict; non-zero-sum supports trading and cooperation |
| **Pareto efficiency** | No outcome can improve someone without hurting another | Trading games: ensure trades create surplus |
| **Subgame / sequential games** | Order of moves matters; later players know more | First-move advantage; turn order balancing |
| **Backward induction** | Reasoning from the end state | Predictable endgames; explains "last turn" behavior |
| **Auctions** | Bidding mechanisms | Bidding games, winner's curse, bid fatigue |
| **Signaling** | Costly actions reveal private information | Bluffing, hidden information |
| **Commitment** | Binding choices | Pre-commit mechanics (simultaneous planning) |
| **Tragedy of the commons** | Overuse of shared resource | Shared map resources, public goods |

### 10.7 Sources of uncertainty (Costikyan)

Greg Costikyan's *Uncertainty in Games* (MIT Press, 2013) argues that uncertainty is central to games: if the outcome were known, there would be no game. He catalogs the sources of uncertainty a design can use; the following paraphrases the types commonly attributed to the book (confirm the exact list in the text before quoting):
| Source | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Randomness** | Dice, shuffled cards, random events | Dice, loot drops |
| **Hidden information** | Players do not know all state | Poker hands, fog of war |
| **Player unpredictability** | Opponents' choices | Fighting game reads, negotiation |
| **Analytic complexity** | Too many possibilities to compute | Chess, Go |
| **Performance uncertainty** | Will my hands/skill execute? | Platformer jump, sports |
| **Solver's uncertainty** | Not knowing the solution | Puzzles, mysteries |
| **Narrative anticipation** | What happens next in a story | Branching story |

Use as a design lens: **for each game, name where the uncertainty comes from**, and check that it is the kind that supports the experience (a skill-driven game should not have its uncertainty mostly from dice). See `03`, section 9, on input and output randomness.

## 11. Worked Examples

### 11.1 Fixing a "bullet sponge" shooter encounter

Symptoms: players report that enemies feel unfair and tedious. Diagnosis steps:
1. Telemetry: time-to-kill (TTK) per enemy is high; damage taken is spiky.
2. Observation: all enemies target the player at once from multiple directions.
3. Fixes: introduce attack tokens (N = 2 ranged, 2 melee), vary roles (a sniper with a tell, a flanker with an audio cue, a tank with a weak point), reduce health on fodder, increase aggression of attacks rather than health, add a rest beat after peaks. Re-test TTK and damage distribution.
4. Keep a record of metrics before and after (`12`).

### 11.2 A stealth awareness model

Spec: Guard vision cone 70 degrees, range 12 m (placeholders). Awareness fills at a rate scaled by distance, player stance (crouch halves rate), and light level; decays when out of view. Thresholds: 0.3 suspicious (turn and investigate), 0.7 searching (move to last known position), 1.0 alert (combat, call allies). Audio: low rising tone at suspicion, distinctive sting at alert, music state change. UI: meter above guard; accessible alternative with icons and vibration. Test: players predict guard behavior; adjust fill rates until 80% of testers report "I understood why I was seen" **[target is a heuristic; choose your own]**.

### 11.3 Procedural dungeon floor

Goal: 10 rooms, 1 start, 1 exit, 1 shop, 1 mini-boss, 2 optional treasure rooms.
1. Build a 4x4 grid; choose a random walk from top-left to bottom-right (the guaranteed path).
2. Assign room roles along the path by slot: start, fight, fight, rest/shop, fight, mini-boss, exit.
3. Fill path rooms from the chunk library by role and connector; add side rooms for treasure with a gate requiring a key found on the path.
4. Budget: threat points per room by depth; spawn from authored enemy groups.
5. Validate: flood fill reachability, key before lock, resource minimums; reject and re-roll on failure (max 5 attempts, then authored fallback).
6. Log seed and generator version.

### 11.4 Adaptive music state map

States: Explore (layers 1-2), Tension (add layer 3 and percussion), Combat (full mix), Victory (stinger then Explore). Transitions: Explore -> Tension on first perception, at next bar; Tension -> Combat on first hit; Combat -> Explore when no threats for 8 seconds, fading over 4 bars (placeholders). Accessibility: captions for stingers when they carry information; a toggle for music intensity.

### 11.5 Detecting a dominant strategy with a payoff matrix

Three ability choices (Shield, Dash, Burst). Simulated pairwise win rates show Burst beats Shield 70%, Dash beats Burst 60%, Shield beats Dash 55%: an intransitive cycle (healthy). If Burst beats both, it dominates: add cost or counter. Method: build the pairwise matrix from simulations or logs, check for rows with all values above 50%.

## 12. Checklists

### 12.1 Enemy design checklist
- [ ] Every enemy has a role and a one-sentence question it asks.
- [ ] Each attack has a tell on at least two channels.
- [ ] A fair counter exists; at least two counters exist per role.
- [ ] Silhouettes and sounds are distinguishable at a glance.
- [ ] Aggression is managed (tokens, pacing); crowd size is limited.
- [ ] Failure states (stuck, no path, lost target) have fallbacks.
- [ ] Debug overlay shows state, target, perception.
- [ ] Difficulty changes behavior, not just health.

### 12.2 Boss checklist
- [ ] Phases change the question, not just numbers.
- [ ] Each pattern has tell, counter, and opening.
- [ ] Retry loop is short.
- [ ] Fight teaches itself; no unfair one-shots on first exposure.
- [ ] Hitboxes match visuals.
- [ ] Audio and visual cues for phase changes.

### 12.3 Procedural generation checklist
- [ ] Unit of content, guaranteed experience and variation axes defined.
- [ ] Expressive range plotted; not oatmeal.
- [ ] Solvability guaranteed by construction or verified.
- [ ] Pacing skeleton and budgets exist.
- [ ] Seeds versioned; RNG streams separated.
- [ ] Mass simulation and bot tests run nightly.
- [ ] Authored pieces where attention is highest.

### 12.4 Audio checklist
- [ ] Every critical piece of information has an audio and a non-audio channel.
- [ ] Priority and ducking rules defined.
- [ ] Music state map and transitions specified.
- [ ] Volume sliders per category; subtitles and captions.
- [ ] Variation for frequent sounds; consistency for key ones.
- [ ] Tested on multiple outputs and under busy conditions.

### 12.5 Game theory checklist
- [ ] No strictly dominant strategy among the meaningful options.
- [ ] Guessing situations have information and bounded stakes.
- [ ] Social dilemmas are intentional, with communication and consequences defined.
- [ ] Sources of uncertainty named and appropriate to the experience.
